How the two dials are built

The dome is raised without touching the chin: the skull is drawn as two half-ellipses that share a vertical radius only at the leftmost point, so the top half can run at 54 or 56 while the bottom half stays at the approved 48. That leftmost point sits behind the body stem, so the curvature break where the two halves meet is never visible. Raising the dome does carry the upper mouth corner forward a couple of units — a taller ellipse meets the same height further out — which lengthens the top jaw slightly and reads as a mild overbite. The lip is a fillet: the arc stops short of the corner, the jaw line restarts past it, and a quadratic bridges the two while still leaning on the original point, so the corner softens without the mouth changing size.

Why it isn't just a scale

Enlarging the head proportionally — which is what bowl 54 did back in round two — grows it up and left as well as right, so it swallows the stem and the whole mark has to be re-centred in the tile. Pinning the left edge and stretching only the horizontal radius keeps the head's join with the body identical, and because the throat vertex is held where it is while the mouth corners travel forward, the wedge lengthens on its own: a bigger head and a deeper maw out of one number. The outline stays an even weight because the ellipse is drawn as an ellipse, not as a squashed circle — a transform would have made the vertical strokes heavier than the horizontal ones.

The snout tip, made flush

First cut of these had a visible step where the top of the head meets the front of the mouth. The cause was in how the teeth get re-laid: each tooth was being scaled about the midpoint of its base, and the first upper tooth starts life sitting on the mouth corner — so on a longer jaw it walked about a unit inboard, and its rounded join and the corner's rounded join stopped sharing an outer edge. Two 5-wide haloes a unit apart read as a notch. Teeth are now anchored on their leading edge instead, which pins that first tooth back on the corner. Under that sat a second, older fault, and this one is in the approved file too: the black inner shape carries a hairline 0.8 stroke to cover the antialiasing seam against the orange, and at a corner as sharp as the mouth's the default miter join runs that stroke about 0.7 units past the point. The result was a black spike stabbing across the snout band — the tick on the outside of the tip. The join is now round, so it cannot overshoot, and the dome's edge runs into the front of the mouth as one line. Regenerating at head 48 still reproduces the approved geometry byte-for-byte; only that one join attribute differs.

The two tooth builds

The marks above use closed teeth — each tooth is a shape with its own outline, so a second line runs alongside the jaw. That's fine at a matched weight and it's what gives the approved version its density. The iterations below use open teeth: two edges meeting at a point, no base, laid over the finished outline so they join the jaw instead of doubling it. That's what makes a genuinely thin tooth possible — at a closed build, dropping the tooth weight just turns the mouth into scribble. Both builds are in the generator; nothing had to be given up.

My read

Heavy body — a 7 silhouette against a 2.5 tooth. The gap between the weights is what makes the teeth read as teeth rather than as more outline, and it's the only one of the six where the mouth still says something at 24px. Sharper is the same idea at the weight you already liked. I'd skip Fangs.
